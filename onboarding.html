Onboarding shouldn't feel like figuring it out alone

At Ambev Tech, new designers got a laptop, a dense wiki, and a "Buddy" who rarely had time. I designed a simple app to replace that with something that actually works — one task at a time.

Part One

Everyone had a Buddy. Almost no one had a guide.

I was the Design Lead for the Sales vertical at Ambev Tech, leading a team of twelve designers. On paper, every new hire got a "Buddy" — a colleague assigned to help them find their footing in their first weeks.

In practice, that person rarely had the bandwidth. The new hire was handed a laptop and, mostly, left to figure it out.

There was a wiki. It lived inside Microsoft Teams, and it was dense enough that finding anything in it was its own onboarding task.

I noticed this because I kept living it, over and over. Every time someone joined my team, I became the onboarding — walking each new designer through the same tools, the same context, the same culture, one conversation at a time.

Part Two

From "just part of the job" to a design problem

At some point I stopped treating this as something I just did and started treating it as something I could actually design. I talked to my own leadership and to the People & Culture team to understand what information genuinely mattered in someone's first weeks.

Then I talked to my colleagues — the designers who had already lived through it — about what they wished they'd had on day one, and what had actually stuck with them.

A pattern showed up fast: nobody needed more information. They needed it broken into pieces they could act on, in an order that made sense, with a person attached to it instead of a folder.

The Idea

A checklist, a Buddy, and a feedback loop

What came out of those conversations was deliberately simple. The area manager builds a checklist for the new hire — meet the team, confirm access to every tool, read about the culture, meet your Buddy, book your first 1:1. The new hire checks tasks off as they go, so both sides can see exactly where things stand without a single status meeting.

At the two-week mark, the new hire writes a short retrospective on how onboarding actually went. That feedback goes straight back to the managers — so the process gets better with every person who goes through it, instead of staying frozen the way the old wiki was.

How it works

Three simple moves

01

Set the path

The manager builds a checklist tailored to the role and the team — once, in minutes, instead of repeating it from memory each time someone joins.

02

Work through it

The new hire moves through their own tasks at their own pace, always able to see what's done and what's left.

03

Reflect and improve

A two-week retrospective turns one person's experience into a better process for the next person.

Behind the design

How this got made

This one didn't start with a brief. It started with noticing the same problem happen to person after person, and deciding it was worth fixing even though nobody had assigned it to me.

Research

Conversations with leadership and People & Culture on what belongs in onboarding, plus one-on-ones with my own designers on what they actually needed in their first weeks.

Design

Paper wireframes first, then a Figma prototype — a task-based flow for the new hire, a setup flow for the manager, and a retrospective to close the loop.

Reality

I left Ambev before the prototype was complete. A handful of screens are still stubs — and that's part of the story, not something to hide.

Part Four

Where I'd take it next: adaptive, not just simple

The version I designed at Ambev was intentionally simple — the same checklist for every new hire, built once by a manager. That was the right first version. It's not the last one.

The direction I actually want to build toward is an onboarding that adapts itself — where tasks, pacing and even what the Buddy is nudged to check in on shift based on the person's role, team, and how they're actually progressing, not a fixed template. The retrospective data wouldn't just close one onboarding out; it would shape the next one automatically. That's the "adaptive" in the name — and it's the version I'd want AI doing quietly in the background, instead of a manager doing it by hand.
